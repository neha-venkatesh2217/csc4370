Black widow spiders belong to the genus Latrodectus, which includes over 30 species worldwide, such as the North American southern (L. mactans), northern (L. variolus), and western (L. hesperus) black widows, as well as the Australian redback (L. hasselti) and brown widows. dult females are typically shiny black with a distinctive red, orange, or yellow hourglass-shaped marking on the underside of the abdomen, while males are smaller, often gray, brown, or red, and less conspicuous. Females measure about 1.5 inches (38 mm) including leg span, whereas males are roughly half that size. Juveniles are pale white or orange and develop their characteristic markings as they mature.

Black widows are found on almost every continent except Antarctica, favoring warm, dark, and secluded areas such as under logs, in drain pipes, sheds, and agricultural structures. They are commonly associated with urban and rural environments, often building irregular, tangled cobwebs close to the ground.

Black widow venom is neurotoxic and approximately 15 times more potent than rattlesnake venom, affecting nerve cells. Bites can cause symptoms such as nausea, muscle pain, and paralysis of the diaphragm, but fatalities are extremely rare. In the United States, around 2,500 bites are reported annually, with no deaths since 1983.
